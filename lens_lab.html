Research Objectives: Probing Model Robustness

As a Student Researcher, I am investigating the boundaries of SmolVLA and Imitation Learning. Our current research focuses on identifying the specific variables—color, geometry, and spatial placement—that trigger model failure or success in generalized environments.

  • Data Efficiency: Quantifying the minimum threshold of expert demonstrations required for stable task completion to reduce the overhead of Imitation Learning.
  • Feature Generalization: Testing how VLA models weigh visual features (e.g., distinguishing an object by color vs. shape) and their ability to generalize to novel object placements.
  • Algorithmic Implementation: Utilizing Lerobot to deploy and compare ACT (Action Chunking with Transformers), SmolVLA, and larger VLA models on SO101 robotic platforms.

Scientific Methodology

We are systematically perturbing the environment to observe the model's policy robustness. By keeping the same task but varying the objects and their placement, we aim to understand what VLA models are generalizing, and what concepts they struggle to learn.

Reflection

Working in the LENS Lab was intimidating in a way I hadn't experienced before. Surrounded by extremely talented graduate students and professors tackling advanced topics in imitation learning, I hit a steep vocabulary barrier almost immediately. There was an overwhelming amount of code, concepts, and background knowledge I had to absorb before I even understood what questions to ask, let alone how to answer them.

Weekly meetings with my professor and grad student mentors added real accountability to that learning curve, and at times the pressure to make progress on difficult, open-ended problems felt like more than I could handle. But pushing through that pressure taught me how to learn new concepts quickly, how to break a problem that initially seems too big into something tractable, and that intimidation is usually a sign I'm about to learn something, not a sign I don't belong.

As I grew more comfortable in the lab, I found myself naturally stepping into a coordinating role — organizing meetings, delegating tasks, and helping keep a group of busy undergrads on schedule and moving toward shared deadlines. That responsibility taught me as much about leadership and accountability as the research itself did. The LENS Lab pushed me further outside my comfort zone than almost anything else in my undergraduate career, and I came out of it a far more resilient, self-directed engineer.
